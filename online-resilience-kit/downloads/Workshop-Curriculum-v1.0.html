Introduction for Facilitators

This curriculum is designed to be delivered in three standalone sessions of approximately 2.5–3 hours each. Sessions can be run on consecutive weekends or spread over a month. Each session is self-contained enough that attendees who miss one can still benefit from others.

Materials Needed (All Sessions)

  • This curriculum guide (facilitator copy, printed)
  • Participant handout packet (print one per person — Sections marked PARTICIPANT)
  • Quick-cards set (pre-printed and laminated — 1 set per 2 participants)
  • Whiteboard or flip chart + markers
  • Extension cords and power strips
  • Optional: projector or large monitor

SESSION 1 — FOUNDATIONS AND RADIO BASICS
Duration: 2.5–3 hours   |   Maximum Participants: 20 (larger groups work but reduce hands-on time)   |   Prerequisites: None — this is the entry point

▶ SESSION 1 LEARNING OBJECTIVES

By the end of this session, participants will be able to:

  1. Explain why offline communication networks matter and when they're needed
  2. Identify the three pillars of community communications resilience
  3. Operate a basic VHF/UHF handheld radio (power on, frequency, transmit, receive)
  4. Perform a proper radio check-in using standard procedure
  5. Locate and use the Emergency Frequency Quick-Card
SEGMENT 1.1 — WELCOME AND INTRODUCTIONS — 20 Minutes

Facilitator Actions

  • Welcome participants; introduce yourself and any co-facilitators
  • Have participants introduce themselves (name, neighborhood, any radio experience)
  • Display or draw the three-pillar diagram: Mesh / Radio / Physical

★ ICE BREAKER ACTIVITY — “What Would You Miss Most?” (5 minutes)

Ask: “If the internet and cell phones went down right now, what communication would you miss most — and how would you replace it?”

Brief discussion (5 minutes). Record answers on whiteboard.

Key Teaching Point: Most of what we “need” from the internet can be replicated locally with the right preparation. This session is about how.

✎ PARTICIPANT HANDOUT 1.1 — THE CASE FOR RESILIENCE

Three Scenarios Where This Matters

Scenario Description Core Challenge
A — The Ice Storm A major ice storm knocks out power for 10 days. Cell towers run on generator for 6 hours, then go dark. How do you find out who needs help? How do you coordinate resources?
B — The Earthquake A 6.5 magnitude earthquake disrupts fiber optic lines and overwhelms cell networks with traffic. Official emergency broadcasts are sporadic. Neighbors two blocks away might as well be in another city.
C — The Cyberattack A ransomware attack on regional ISPs knocks out internet for a major metropolitan area for 72 hours. 911 dispatch is impacted. How does your neighborhood self-organize?

Discussion Questions

  1. Which scenario feels most realistic for your community?
  2. What resources do you already have that could help?
  3. Who in your neighborhood would you turn to first?
SEGMENT 1.2 — UNDERSTANDING THE THREE PILLARS — 25 Minutes

✎ FACILITATOR NOTE

Draw or display the three pillars as a visual throughout this segment. Keep the diagram visible on the whiteboard for the entire session.

Pillar 1 — Mesh Networks (8 minutes)

  • Definition: Devices talk directly to each other — no internet required
  • Self-healing: If one node goes down, traffic routes around it
  • Range: LoRa mesh can reach 1–15 km depending on terrain
  • Text-based messaging, GPS position sharing, limited data

▶ DEMONSTRATION

If Meshtastic devices are available, turn on two and show a message being sent without any internet or cell connection. Have a participant send a message.

Pillar 2 — Radio Communications (10 minutes)

  • Radio has worked for 100 years without internet
  • Multiple layers: handheld (local), mobile (neighborhood), HF (regional/national)
  • Licensed vs. unlicensed bands — brief overview
  • Why amateurs are the backbone of emergency comms

▶ DEMONSTRATION

Power on a radio. Scan for local repeaters. Demonstrate a radio check.

Pillar 3 — Physical Distribution (7 minutes)

  • When everything electronic fails, humans are the network
  • Runners, bulletin boards, printed materials
  • Pre-agreed meeting places and times
  • The oldest and most reliable system

▶ GROUP ACTIVITY

Ask the group: “Where would you put a community bulletin board in your neighborhood that would reach the most people? Could everyone walk to it?”

SEGMENT 1.3 — HANDS-ON RADIO BASICS — 45 Minutes

Materials: 4+ handheld radios (Baofeng UV-5R or similar)

Part A — Radio Orientation (15 minutes)

Walk through with all radios powered on:

  1. Power button and volume control
  2. Channel/frequency display
  3. PTT (Push-to-Talk) button
  4. Monitor/scan function
  5. Squelch — what it is and how to set it
  6. Menu navigation — basic settings

✎ PARTICIPANT HANDOUT 1.3A — RADIO ANATOMY LABELS

[Simple labeled diagram of a handheld radio with arrows pointing to each control. Print one per participant and have them label the parts as the facilitator walks through each one.]

Part B — Standard Radio Procedure (15 minutes)

Standard Format

Step Action Example / Notes
1 Listen before transmitting Wait at least 3 seconds to confirm channel is clear
2 Identify yourself and destination “[Your call sign/name], [destination call sign/name]”
3 Keep transmissions short Say what you need; avoid rambling
4 Say “over” When waiting for response
5 Say “out” When finished with the exchange
6 Say “break break break” For emergencies — interrupts all traffic immediately

Role Play Exchanges (Pairs Practice)

Exchange Type Scenario
1 Routine Request for information about road conditions
2 Urgent Reporting a neighbor who needs a welfare check
3 Emergency Reporting a structure fire with people inside

Part C — Phonetic Alphabet Drill (15 minutes)

Distribute Quick-Card 1. Practice saying names using phonetics:

  • Facilitator says a word; participants spell it using phonetics
  • Then do locations: street names, neighborhood names
  • Finally: practice call signs

▶ ACTIVITY — Speed Round

Random letters called out; participants respond with phonetic words as fast as possible. Increase pace each round to build reflex recall.

SEGMENT 2.2 — MESH NETWORKING DEEP DIVE — 30 Minutes

Part A — How Meshtastic Works (15 minutes)

Concept Plain-Language Explanation
LoRa Technology Long Range radio — like Wi-Fi but much longer range, low data rate
Flood Routing Each device receives and retransmits messages to extend coverage
Store-and-Forward Messages held at a node until they can be passed along
No Infrastructure No internet, no servers, no accounts needed — fully standalone
GPS Position Sharing Built in and optional; share or hide your location
Message Size Text messaging only (no voice) — up to ~228 bytes per message

▶ VISUAL DEMONSTRATION

Use 2–4 Meshtastic devices placed around the room. Show messages routing through intermediate nodes. Point out the node list on the app to illustrate the mesh forming in real time.

Part B — Network Planning Exercise (15 minutes)

▶ GROUP ACTIVITY — “Map Your Neighborhood Mesh”

Materials: Large printed neighborhood map (or hand-drawn), sticky notes, markers

Instructions (groups of 4–5):

  1. Groups of 4–5 people take a map section
  2. Mark high points (hills, tall buildings, church steeples, water towers)
  3. Mark potential node locations (someone who could host a node)
  4. Identify coverage gaps
  5. Note where the most vulnerable community members live

Discussion: What's the minimum number of nodes to cover your neighborhood? Where are the critical relay points?

SEGMENT 2.3 — HANDS-ON MESHTASTIC — 45 Minutes

Materials: 4+ Meshtastic devices, smartphones with Meshtastic app

Part A — Device Setup (20 minutes)

Walk through step by step (all participants follow along):

  1. Install Meshtastic app (or use pre-installed version from USB)
  2. Power on device — observe LED indicators
  3. Pair via Bluetooth
  4. Set region: United States
  5. Set device name (use neighborhood + number, e.g., “Riverside-07”)
  6. Confirm channel: LongFast (default) or community-preset channel
  7. Send a test message to the group

✎ PARTICIPANT HANDOUT 2.3A — MESHTASTIC SETUP CHECKLIST

☐ Step Confirmed By
☐App installed and open 
☐Bluetooth enabled on phone 
☐Device powered on (LED indicator active) 
☐Paired to device (appears in app) 
☐Region set to United States 
☐Device name set 
☐Channel confirmed (LongFast or community channel) 
☐Test message sent and received 
☐GPS enabled (optional — confirm if desired) 

Part B — Range Test Exercise (25 minutes)

Pair up participants. Give each pair a Meshtastic device. Send pairs to different locations:

  • Inside the building (various rooms)
  • Outside (various distances)
  • One pair stays as “base station”

Each pair sends a message every 5 minutes with their location and signal quality. Record results on whiteboard map.

✎ FACILITATOR NOTE — Debrief Prompt

What affected range? Lead discussion around: wall composition, distance, elevation changes, physical obstacles. Reinforce that node placement on rooftops or upper floors dramatically improves coverage.

SEGMENT 2.4 — THE USB KIT — 30 Minutes

Part A — USB Kit Tour (15 minutes)

Distribute USB drives (one per participant or group). Walk through the folder structure together:

Folder Contents How to Use
📁 MANUALS/ Field manual PDF Open with any PDF viewer; navigate offline
📁 MAPS/ Offline map files Open in OsmAnd or Maps.me (no internet needed)
📁 SOFTWARE/ Meshtastic flasher, Briar, Kiwix Install from USB; no download required
📁 MEDIA/ Training video clips, quick-card PDFs Play locally; print quick-cards from PDF
📁 DATA/ Frequency list, contact directory format Reference during operations; keep updated
📁 TEMPLATES/ Message forms, log sheets Print as needed; fill by hand in field

⚠ KEY TEACHING POINTS — USB Kit

  • These USB drives work with no internet
  • Keep one at home; keep one in your go-bag
  • Update on the first Sunday of each month at community update sessions
  • The Kiwix app + Wikipedia ZIM = offline encyclopedia for medical, technical, and legal reference

Part B — Offline Resources Practice (15 minutes)

Participants practice independently:

  1. Task 1: Navigate to your neighborhood on the offline map
  2. Task 2: Find the field manual and navigate to the frequency reference
  3. Task 3: Open Kiwix and search for “first aid” (or similar practical topic)
  4. Task 4: Locate and print-preview the information runner message form
SEGMENT 2.5 — DATA SECURITY BASICS — 15 Minutes

✎ PARTICIPANT HANDOUT 2.5 — DATA SECURITY QUICK GUIDE

What to Store How to Store What to Avoid
Public docs (manual, maps, frequencies) Plain files on USB Names and addresses stored together
Community contacts 7-Zip AES-256 encrypted archive Passwords, financial information
Mesh channel key Share verbally or on paper — in person only Posting online or in plain text files

Additional topics to cover in this segment:

  • What NOT to put on community USB drives: passwords, financial info, medical records, personal identifiers
  • How to create an encrypted archive using 7-Zip AES-256 — live demonstration
  • Meshtastic channel encryption — AES-256 pre-shared key setup
  • Physical security: do not leave USB drives unattended at public events

SEGMENT 3.2 — COMMUNITY ORGANIZATION STRUCTURE — 30 Minutes

Part A — Roles and Responsibility Assignment (15 minutes)

Present the six core roles (from Section 7 of the field manual):

# Role Primary Responsibility Assigned To
1 Network Coordinator Overall coordination of the community network; primary contact for external agencies  
2 Node Custodians Maintain mesh nodes; ensure power and connectivity; report outages  
3 Radio Operators Licensed operators who manage voice radio traffic and nets  
4 Information Runners Physical message delivery between nodes when electronic comms are unavailable  
5 Supply Custodian Track and maintain shared equipment inventory; manage go-box  
6 Training Coordinator Schedule sessions, onboard new participants, coordinate USB updates  

▶ GROUP ACTIVITY — Role Matching

Each participant receives a card describing a scenario/skill set. They self-select or are placed into roles based on skills and interest. Not everyone needs a formal role — many people are simply “network participants.” Fill in the community roles table together on the whiteboard. Capture in the participant directory template.

Part B — The Neighborhood Cell Structure (15 minutes)

Explain cells: 10–20 households, one block captain, direct communication with neighbors.

▶ ACTIVITY — “Draw Your Cell”

Participants sketch their immediate neighborhood (their block/street/cul-de-sac). Mark:

  • Their own home
  • Households they already know
  • Households with elderly/disabled residents (priority for check-in)
  • Potential bulletin board locations
  • Best route to the community hub
SEGMENT 3.3 — COMMUNICATION TREE BUILD — 25 Minutes

Explain the tree structure: each person calls/radios 3–5 others; they each call 3–5 more. A message can reach 100 people in 2–3 “hops.”

▶ GROUP ACTIVITY — “Build the Tree”

Using sticky notes on a wall or whiteboard:

  1. Place facilitator at top
  2. Each participant places their note connected to who would contact them
  3. Fill gaps — who has no one to call them?
  4. Identify single points of failure (someone who is the only link to 10 people)
  5. Add redundant connections

Discussion: What happens when someone in the tree is unavailable? Plan for substitutes before it matters.

Take a photo of the completed tree. Transcribe to the template in TEMPLATES/ folder on USB.

Facilitator Resources

Common Questions and Answers

Q: Do I need a license to use a mesh network?

No. Meshtastic operates in the unlicensed 915 MHz ISM band. No FCC license is required.

Q: What if someone in my neighborhood refuses to participate?

You cannot compel participation. Focus on those who are willing. Having even 20% of households connected provides significant benefit for the whole community.

Q: Is this legal?

Yes. All technologies covered in this curriculum are legal when used as described. Amateur radio requires a license for licensed bands. FRS/GMRS are legal with appropriate licensing. Mesh networks in the ISM band require no license.

Q: What about privacy?

Meshtastic messages on the default channel are not encrypted. Use the community channel key for sensitive communications. USB drives should not contain sensitive personal information in unencrypted form.

Q: How do we fund this?

Many community resilience networks operate entirely on volunteer time and donated equipment. Some obtain grants through local emergency management offices, community foundations, or mutual aid networks. Equipment costs can be shared — one community node can serve 20+ households.
